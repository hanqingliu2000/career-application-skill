# Career evidence base and interview

Use this workflow when the user requests an evidence base or asks to integrate their career materials. Agree on or infer an appropriate storage location and scope from the request; do not collect unrelated personal information. Request access to past resumes, cover letters, application responses, portfolios or other materials the user wants included. Inventory what is actually available and identify missing or inaccessible items without assuming the collection is complete. Preserve original files and previously submitted versions; extract and synthesize rather than replacing them.

Organize a maintainable evidence base around the applicant's profile, chronological roles and education, projects and accomplishments, skills and credentials, career goals and constraints, and open questions. Structure can adapt to the volume and existing workspace; avoid turning copies of old resumes into a second source of truth. For each substantial claim retain its source, date or confirmation status, individual contribution, collaborators, context, result and measurement boundaries where known. Distinguish a source's assertion from something independently verified. Separate aspirations from completed results, prototype from deployment, and individual from team achievements. Do not infer dates, level, employment status, work authorization or credentials. Preserve conflicting statements with their sources and ask about material conflicts; update confirmed facts without repeatedly asking the same question. Avoid capturing sensitive details that are not needed for the requested work.

## Interview for missing experience

The `grilling` skill is required for the detailed interview. Locate and invoke it when the user requests experience exploration. If it is not available, explain the missing dependency and pause the interview until it is installed; continue independent work. Adapt its decision-tree method to evidence gathering: do not suggest or infer personal facts, even if its general instructions recommend answers. First examine existing evidence and identify the most useful unknowns. In each round ask a manageable, numbered group of questions whose prerequisites are settled; wait for answers before dependent follow-ups. Prioritize context and problem, individual decisions and actions, collaborators, constraints, delivery stage, outcomes, metric definitions and limits, and what can be shared publicly. Offer answer structures or prompts, not recommended facts or leading answers. Don't ask the user to discover facts already available in provided sources. Stop when the agreed scope has sufficient evidence or the user pauses; do not manufacture questions. Record user-confirmed additions and dates in the evidence base, distinguish inference from confirmation, and preserve any conflict for clarification.

For later materials, consult the relevant current facts and the user's latest corrections. Do not promote a claimed outcome or private assumption into a verified fact. Only ask questions whose answers would materially improve accuracy, fit, or the requested deliverable.
